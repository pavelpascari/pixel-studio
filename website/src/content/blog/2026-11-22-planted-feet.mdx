Move a walking sprite across a web page and odds are it skates. The legs say one speed, the movement says another,
and the feet slide over the ground like socks on a polished floor. Animators fix this by hand, frame by frame. Pixel
Studio has no frames to fix: a character is a JSON description, and the engine computes its walk. That engine has to
get the feet right by construction and tell the page the exact speed to move the sprite at.

This post follows one character, Ember the fox, from a skeleton to the gallop below.

## A skeleton with roles

Ember is dressed on a **figure**, a reusable skeleton in `figures/dog.json`. Bones are dots and lines in a 100×100
canvas, drawn in a rest pose standing on the ground. A `rig` then says what each bone is for:

```json title="figures/dog.json (abridged)"
{"id": "thighHindNear", "parent": "body", "from": [33, 60], "to": [39, 71], "limits": [-60, 45]},
...
"rig": {
  "root": "body",
  "legs": [
    {"chain": ["thighHindNear", "shinHindNear", "hockHindNear", "pawHindNear"], "phase": 0, "group": "hind"},
    {"chain": ["armForeNear", "forearmForeNear", "pasternForeNear", "pawForeNear"], "phase": 0.25, "group": "fore"},
    ...
  ],
  "neck": ["neck"], "head": "head", "tail": ["tail", "tail2"]
}
```

The character spec draws shapes on those bones and asks for a motion by name, `{"motion": "gallop"}`. The motion
models in `src/core/motion.js` turn the figure's parameters into ordinary keyframe tracks. The validator receives the
gallop as plain arrays of angles, and no code after it knows a figure was involved.

## Walking on a treadmill

The central decision: the character stays inside its canvas and walks on a treadmill. The ground moves backwards
under it, and the page moves the sprite. For each sample of the cycle, each leg works out where its toe should be.
During the first `duty` fraction of its cycle the foot is planted, and the toe slides backwards at a constant rate,
the same speed as the treadmill:

```js title="src/core/motion.js"
if (s < duty) {
  const k = s / duty;
  return { toe: [l.toe0[0] + stride * (0.5 - k), l.toe0[1]], /* … */ };
}
const k = (s - duty) / (1 - duty);
const lift = (m.lift ?? 3) * Math.sin(Math.PI * Math.pow(k, 0.75));
return { toe: [l.toe0[0] + stride * (-0.5 + minJerk(k)), l.toe0[1] - lift], /* … */ };
```

In the air the foot travels forward on a minimum-jerk curve (`10k³ − 15k⁴ + 6k⁵`), which eases in and out, and
lifts on a sine skewed toward the start of the swing. With the toe targets in place, the speed is arithmetic: a
planted foot sweeps `stride` units in `duty × cycle time` seconds, so

```js
const speed = stride / (duty * cycleT);   // canvas units per second
```

Ember's walk sweeps 27 units in 0.65 of a 0.8 s cycle: 51.92 units per second, the number baked into the bundle.

## Solving the body around the feet

Toes on targets are the start. The engine then solves each sample from scratch, in this order:

1. **Hips.** Each leg group (`hind`, `fore`) raises or lowers its end of the body with the support its planted feet
   give. A walk is highest mid-stance, like vaulting over a stiff leg; a run (`bounce: "spring"`) is lowest
   mid-stance. With two groups, the difference between them sets the pitch, so the fox's back rocks as its front and hind legs
   alternate.
2. **Reach.** If a leg cannot reach its toe at `reach` (0.97) of its length, the solver lowers that group's hips as far as
   needed, up to three times. Wide steps lower the body, as they do in life.
3. **Head.** `steady` pulls the head back toward its rest position, and the neck solver puts it there. The fox's
   head glides while its body bobs.
4. **Legs.** The law of cosines solves two-bone legs (the goose, the human) in closed form, keeping the knee on the
   side it bends to in the rest pose. The dog's four-bone legs use CCD, an iterative solver that turns one joint at
   a time toward the target, for 64 iterations within each joint's `limits`.

<MarginNote kind="field" numbered>CCD can wedge a leg against a joint limit and stop short. The fix in `motion.js` is blunt: if the toe misses by more than 0.05 units, try again from a seed with every joint bent to the middle of its limits, and keep whichever result is closer.</MarginNote>
The engine runs the whole cycle twice and records the second pass. CCD starts from its previous answer, so the first
pass warms it up and the recorded loop ends where it begins.

Ask for the impossible, say a stride longer than the legs, and expansion warns with the leg and frame: `thighHindNear: foot misses its mark by 0.8 units at frame 3.25`. The test suite runs
every figure in `figures/` and fails on any such miss.

## From canvas units to CSS pixels

A bundle stores speed in canvas units (the figures' canvas is 100 units tall). The runtime converts to CSS pixels on
request, using the sprite's drawn height:

```js title="packages/runtime/src/pixel-sprite.js"
pxPerSecond(name = this._anim) {
  const a = this._bundle?.animations[name];
  if (!a?.speed || !this._css) return null;
  return (a.speed * this._css.height) / (this._bundle.size?.[1] ?? 100);
}
```

`_css.height` can differ from the `size` attribute. The runtime scales each sprite pixel to a whole number of device
pixels, so a sprite may draw at a different size than requested. Converting from the drawn height keeps the feet
planted either way.

Movement also has to follow playback rate. A sprite has a `rate` attribute, and `effectiveRate` multiplies in the
current state-machine node's rate and the burst of speed it gets from rapid clicks. `<pixel-stage>` walks characters
around a page and scales every movement by it:

```js title="packages/runtime/src/pixel-stage.js"
// Faster playback (a boost, a burst) means proportionally faster movement: the feet stay planted.
return ((name && this.sprite.pxPerSecond(name)) || this.H * (role === 'run' ? 1.4 : 0.5)) * this.sprite.effectiveRate;
```

The rate control under the demo above does the same, and the strips speed up with the legs.

Hand-keyed characters carry a speed too. Moss the beetle has frames keyed by hand on a 16-cell grid and declares
its own: a foot moves one cell (6.25 units) back over four frames at 14 fps, so its walk says
`"speed": 21.875`. Pip, below, stands on a front-view figure. A walk toward the viewer has no sideways travel, so
its `stride` is forced to 0 and its bundle has no speed.

## A gallop measured from 1887

Ember's walk and trot take their numbers from Maes et al. (2008), which measured five dogs from 0.4 to 10 m/s and
published fits for cycle time, duty factor and stride. The gallop comes from older photographs. In 1887 Eadweard
Muybridge published *Animal Locomotion*, sequences of exposures with a chronograph recording the interval between
them. The Boston Public Library's scans carry "No known copyright restrictions".

Plates 708 to 710 show racing hounds galloping. On plate 710 each phase recurs five frames later, and the
catalogue gives 49 ms between exposures: a 0.245 s stride. Ember's gallop is 6 frames at 24 fps, 0.25 s. In each
cycle a paw is down in one frame of five, so the duty is about 0.2 (the preset uses 0.22, the mean of the three
plates rounded). Both hinds land close together and the fores land about half a cycle later, one frame apart. Each cycle has two
flights, one with the legs stretched out and one with them gathered under the body.

<MarginNote kind="field">At those exposures the legs blur, and near and far legs look the same. The measurement file records that the leading side was not visible and that the rotary order of the fores is assumed, not verified.</MarginNote>
The plates give times but no distances, so the gallop's stride is the Maes fit for stride length at 6.5 m/s, times
the duty. `references/dog.json` logs each value with its source and the table, frame or formula it came from, and
`npm test` fails if a figure drifts from its log.

The measurements caught two mistakes, one in the catalogue and one in Ember. The catalogue lists plate 706 as a
mastiff trotting, but its support pattern is a slow lateral walk, so the plate now checks `dog.walk`. And the
published numbers showed the old trot moving at 56.7 units per second, about walking pace for a dog of that size.

## Sources with no strings

Poses may come only from public-domain, CC0 or CC BY images and video. Measured numbers may also come from
copyrighted papers, since copyright does not cover facts, but the project copies no figure or text from them.
Share-alike, non-commercial and no-derivatives licences fail the rule, as do motion-capture libraries whose terms
restrict redistribution (Mixamo, ActorCore, Rokoko).

<MarginNote kind="warning">The best gallop clip the research found, a slow-motion greyhound on Wikimedia Commons, is CC BY-SA 4.0 and fails the rule. The notes point to Muybridge's plates 708 to 710 instead, which also record exposure times.</MarginNote>
Because of that rule, the research used 1887 plates and journal tables rather than motion capture. It also measured
one open alternative against the figures, Mesh2Motion's CC0 animated clips. Their leg motion projects well onto a
side view, but keyed clips do not share a ground plane, and the prototype's foot-contact detection read the hind-foot
duty of Mesh2Motion's fox gallop as 0.07.

## Jumps, climbs and what trails behind

The other models use the same treadmill idea. A `jump` jumps in place and reports `launch` and `land` events, so the
page carries the sprite. `<pixel-stage>` reads the flight time between those frames, divided by `effectiveRate`, and
`jumpArc()` solves for the gravity that lands the sprite on target in exactly that time. A `climb` plants the feet on a wall
and sweeps them down, while the page moves the sprite up at `speedY`.

The warnings also show where a model stops working. The gosling in the demo uses the goose figure, and with the
climb left in, its feet miss the wall by up to 6.1 units, with a warning on every sample. Its spec sets
`"climb": null`.

The motion models leave tails and ears alone. A bone with `follow` is a damped spring hanging from its pivot, and it
gets a push when its pivot accelerates or its parent turns. `animate.js` simulates the spring with 8 substeps per
frame, runs a looping animation four times and keeps the last pass, so the loop has no seam. The dog's tail has a
natural frequency of 2 Hz, its tip 2.6 Hz and its ear 4 Hz, so the tail lags behind the body's bounce.

## Seeing it

The figure viewer, `studio/figures.html`, scrolls the ground at each motion's speed and leaves a trail where the feet
touch down. A planted foot should leave one short dotted line that moves away at the ground's speed. A smear
means the stride is wrong, and the tuning advice in the docs starts there: set the stride by eye with the trails on,
then let the validator confirm it. [figures.md](/docs/reference/figures/) documents every parameter, and
[Moving sprites](/docs/guide/moving/) shows how to move a sprite at its own speed.
